The problem

Importance is a question, not a property

Ask who the most important character in the Marvel network is and the answer is Spider-Man, by every measure we have. He has the most links, he is closest to everyone, more shortest paths run through him than through anyone else, and he wins on PageRank too. Four different definitions of importance, one winner.

That unanimity should be suspicious rather than satisfying. The four measures ask genuinely different questions — how many people do you know, how fast can you reach everyone, who has to go through you, who is endorsed by the endorsed — and a network where they all give the same answer is a network where three of them are redundant. So either Marvel really is that simple, or the measures are being dominated by something they share.

They share degree. And week 2 taught us what to do about a number that might just be degree in disguise: shuffle the links while holding every character's degree fixed, and see whether the number survives.

This week we run that test on every character at once. It splits the network into three groups — the characters whose importance is exactly what their popularity predicts, the ones who have less, and a small handful who have dramatically more. The largest surprise in the network is a character with two links, and once you see why, the whole idea of centrality makes more sense.

Method

What we did

Everything path-based here comes out of breadth-first search: stand on a character, everyone they link to is at distance 1, everyone those people link to that you have not seen is at distance 2, and so on. We wrote it ourselves rather than calling networkx, then checked it against networkx from 20 random starting characters. It agrees exactly. Same for PageRank — written as the iteration, validated against the library at three values of α, agreeing to ten decimal places.

Those validations are not ceremony. Both algorithms are short enough that an LLM writes them instantly and plausibly, and both fail in ways that produce numbers rather than errors. A BFS that forgets to check whether it has already seen a node still returns distances, just wrong ones. A PageRank that mishandles the 20 characters whose pages link to nobody still returns a ranking, just one that has quietly leaked probability out of the distribution. Ours sums to 1.000000000 at every α we tried, which is the check that catches it.

def bfs(G, source):
    dist = {source: 0}
    queue = deque([source])
    while queue:
        v = queue.popleft()
        for w in G[v]:
            if w not in dist:            # the guard that makes it a *shortest* path
                dist[w] = dist[v] + 1
                queue.append(w)
    return dist

On the 277-character giant component we compute degree, closeness, harmonic, betweenness and eigenvector centrality, and PageRank on the directed network. Then the null: 200 degree-preserving shuffles, recomputing betweenness and closeness for every character in every shuffle, so each character gets their own distribution and their own z-score.

Our numbers match the ones the course publishes independently: Spider-Man's closeness 0.5948 against a stated 0.59, his mean distance 1.6812 against 1.68, the median character 0.3812 against 0.38, his betweenness 0.2349 against 0.23.

Results

Three kinds of character

Plot betweenness against degree and you get a diagonal. That is the whole story for most of the network — the two have a rank correlation above 0.9, and if all you wanted was a ranking you could stop at degree and save the computation.

Spider-Man's row is the one to sit with. Nearly a quarter of all shortest paths in the Marvel universe pass through him — six times the next character — and it is, to within half a standard deviation, exactly what a randomly wired character with 106 links would have. His dominance is real and it is not information. Anyone with 106 links in this network is the broker of the Marvel universe, whoever they are.

Analysis

Why a character with two links wins

Rockman's z of 7.98 is nearly twice the next largest in the network. He has two links. The second one is the entire explanation.

The Witness has degree 1. His only link is to Rockman. So every shortest path from The Witness to each of the other 275 characters in the giant component runs through Rockman — all 275 of them. That is what betweenness measures, and he collects the lot.

In a shuffled network this almost never happens. The shuffle keeps Rockman at two links but reassigns which two, and the chance that one lands on a character with no other connection is small. His shuffled betweenness averages 0.0002; the real value is 36 times that. He is not well connected. He is a door, and being the only door to somewhere is a different kind of importance from knowing everybody.

Hercules and Black Widow are the same phenomenon at a more intuitive scale. Hercules has 26 links spread across the Avengers, the Hulk's circle, the cosmic characters and Asgard — four regions that do not otherwise talk to each other much. Black Widow's neighbours run Spider-Man, Wolverine, Doctor Strange, Deadpool, Venom, Cloak and Dagger, Hercules, Adam Warlock: no two from the same corner of the universe. Both are bridges rather than members.

And why Black Cat is the opposite

Black Cat has 28 links, more than Hercules, and a third of the betweenness her degree predicts. The reason is that 24 of her 28 neighbours are also Spider-Man's. She lives inside his circle. Every path that might have gone through her has a shortcut through him instead, so her links buy popularity and nothing else. Storm is the same story inside the X-Men.

This is why "well connected" is not a definition of anything. Black Cat and Hercules have comparable degrees and opposite structural roles, and no amount of counting links tells them apart.

Closeness cannot be surprised. We ran the identical test on closeness and almost nothing came out — the real values sit inside the shuffled distributions nearly everywhere, and what few outliers exist are negative.

The reason is in what each measure does with structure. Closeness is an inverse mean distance over 276 other characters, and in a network with diameter 6 where 86% of pairs are two or three steps apart, that mean is squeezed into a narrow band by the degree sequence alone. Betweenness is a sum over paths, where a single bottleneck can dominate. One measure averages structure away; the other concentrates it. If you want brokers, closeness is the wrong instrument — not because it is worse, but because it answers a question this network has no interesting answer to.

The test

Does any of it matter? We removed them and looked

Brokerage is a story about structure, and stories about structure are cheap. If Hercules and Rockman really hold parts of the network together, removing them should break it faster than removing the popular characters. So we took the giant component apart four ways — most links first, highest betweenness first, biggest broker z first, random order — and watched the largest surviving piece.

The honest answer is that it barely matters. Removing the 40 highest-betweenness characters leaves 74% of the network connected in one piece. Removing 40 at random leaves 85%. Targeting helps, but the gap between the best attack we could construct and no strategy at all is eleven percentage points, and neither produces anything like fragmentation.

The single-removal version is starker. The network has only 13 articulation points — characters whose removal disconnects anybody at all — out of 277. The worst is Spider-Man, and taking him out strands five characters. Black Widow strands three. Rockman, our champion broker, strands exactly one: The Witness, the person he is the door for.

So the brokerage is real but local. Rockman genuinely is the only path to The Witness, and that genuinely earns a z of 8 — but "the only path to one person" is its entire extent. The network is held together by redundancy, not by brokers: so many overlapping paths run through the dense middle that removing any individual just reroutes traffic.

This is the most useful thing we found, and it is a caution about our own headline. Betweenness measures how much traffic currently flows through you, not how much damage your absence would do. Those coincide in a sparse, tree-like network. In this one they come apart, and a report using high betweenness to argue "this character is critical infrastructure" would be wrong in a way no centrality number reveals.

Direction

One vote from Spider-Man

Everything above throws away the arrows. PageRank keeps them and adds one idea: a page splits its vote among its out-links, so an endorsement from somewhere selective is worth more than one from somewhere indiscriminate.

Spider-Man's page links to just nine characters. His is the largest vote in the network, divided nine ways, and you can see where it lands.

Black Cat is 17th by in-degree and 5th by PageRank. Mayday Parker is 33rd by in-degree and 7th. Neither is widely mentioned; both are mentioned by the most important page in the network, which mentions almost nobody.

Note what this does to Black Cat. By betweenness she is the most sheltered character in the network, with a third of the brokerage her degree predicts. By PageRank she is fifth most important. Both are correct. They answer different questions — who has to go through you versus who vouches for you — and her position scores one high and the other low. That is the clearest demonstration we found that these are not interchangeable rankings of the same underlying thing.

Two more

Mixing, and the six X-Men

Week 2 found the network mildly disassortative at r = −0.105, and found the degree sequence explains all of it. This week's 200 shuffles confirm it from a larger sample: −0.1217 ± 0.0125, putting the real value 1.3 standard deviations out. The curve shows the same thing in more detail.

The falling line looks like a finding and is not one. A character with 106 links in a network of 277 cannot have high-degree neighbours, because only 37 characters have 20 or more links in the entire network. The hubs link downward because there is nothing else to link to.

The cliques are more fun. The largest fully connected group has eight members, and there are six different ones — all X-Men, all sharing the same six-person core: Betsy Braddock, Cyclops, Emma Frost, Jean Grey, Rachel Summers and Wolverine. The eighth seat rotates between Storm, Cable, Northstar, Jubilee and Phoenix.

That is a tighter result than "the biggest clique is the X-Men." Six characters whose pages all mention each other, sitting inside a looser ring of five more. It is next week's community structure showing through early, and it is the obvious place to point the clustering surplus we have carried since week 2 — the triangles the degree sequence could not explain have to live somewhere, and a six-person mutual-admiration core is what they look like up close.

Conclusion

What we would defend

The finding we would put our name to is that importance here is almost entirely predicted by degree, and the exceptions are worth more than the rule. Of 277 characters, 259 have enough betweenness variance to test at all; 23 of those sit more than two standard deviations from what their degree predicts. Everyone else is their own degree wearing a different measure's name.

The one we would defend hardest is Rockman, because the number and the explanation fit exactly. A z of 8 at two links sounds absurd until you see that one of those links goes to someone with no other links, and then it is obvious and slightly beautiful. Betweenness was designed in 1979 to find exactly this, and it found it in a dataset nobody built for the purpose.

The one we would not defend is the interpretation we nearly wrote. "Rockman is critically important to the network" is what the z-score invites, and the removal experiment says it is false — he is critical to one person. Betweenness measures current traffic, not the cost of absence. We would not have caught that if we had stopped at the centrality table.

Carrying into week 4: the six overlapping 8-cliques, and whether the clustering surplus from week 2 lives inside structures like them. And a question this week raised and could not answer — whether the brokers bridge communities in a formal sense, which is exactly what next week's tools are for. Hercules looks like a yes. Rockman looks like a door to a cupboard.
